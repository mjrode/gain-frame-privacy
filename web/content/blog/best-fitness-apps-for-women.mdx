## Goal 1: Strength training

### 1. Hevy — Best log for running your own program

**Platform:** iOS &amp; Android · **Price:** Free tier; Pro optional · **iOS:** 4.9 stars, 81,000+ ratings

Hevy is a plain, fast workout log, and that's the whole appeal. Build a routine, log sets between rest periods, and watch per-exercise charts show whether your hip thrust is actually progressing or just feels heavy. The free tier is fully usable long-term.

It has no women's programming and no coaching, which is a feature if you already know what you're doing or you're following a written program from elsewhere. The interface asks nothing of you beyond tapping numbers. Strong is the closest rival, compared in [Hevy vs Strong](/blog/hevy-vs-strong/).

**Best for:** progressive overload you can verify week to week. **Limitations:** brings no program of its own; you need a plan before it's useful.

### 2. Ladder — Best coached strength programs

**Platform:** iOS &amp; Android · **Price:** subscription · **iOS:** 4.9 stars, 168,000+ ratings

If the blocker is "I don't know what to do in the weight room," Ladder solves the actual problem. You join a coach-led team, get a structured multi-week strength program with video demos, and log against it. Several of the strength and hybrid programs are run by women coaches, and the programming is real lifting — barbells, dumbbells, progression — rather than light-weight circuits marketed as toning.

The rating volume is high enough that it isn't a niche bet. The cost is the obvious tradeoff, and the team format works better for some people than others.

**Best for:** wanting an actual program written by a coach instead of assembling one. **Limitations:** subscription pricing sits well above a plain logging app; the social team element is not for everyone.

## Goal 2: See whether it's showing up

### 3. GainFrame — Best for tracking visible physique change

**Platform:** [iOS App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/gainframe-progress-photos/id6759252082) · **Price:** Free (25 photos lifetime) with Pro at $5.99/mo or $39.99/yr · **iOS:** 4.9 stars, 39 ratings

Recomposition is the case where every other number lies to you. You're losing fat and adding muscle at roughly the same rate, so the scale sits still for months while your body changes underneath it. [GainFrame](https://gainframe.app) reads a progress photo and returns an estimated body fat percentage, FFMI, a 1–100 score, and ratings across 12 muscle groups, so the flat scale stops being the only feedback you get.

The side-by-side comparison is the part worth the install. Two photos eight weeks apart, aligned, with the estimates next to each other, catches change your eyes have adapted to seeing daily.

**Best for:** recomp, where the scale reports nothing useful. **Limitations:** iOS only, with no Android version; these are AI estimates from photos rather than clinical measurement, and the rating count is small because the app is new. Photos are analyzed without an account and are not stored on a server.

## Goal 3: Nutrition

### 4. MacroFactor — Best nutrition tracker

**Platform:** iOS &amp; Android · **Price:** around $12/mo, cheaper annually · **iOS:** 4.8 stars, 19,000+ ratings

Protein is the lever that decides whether recomp works, and most women eat well under the commonly cited 0.7–1 gram per pound of body weight guidance without tracking it at least for a while. MacroFactor's advantage is its adaptive expenditure model: it reads your weight trend against logged intake and adjusts your calorie target on its own, so a plateau produces a new number instead of a guessing session.

It's also one of the few trackers with no ad layer, no streak guilt, and no shaming design patterns — it reports numbers and moves on. Our [MacroFactor review](/blog/macrofactor-review/) has the full breakdown.

**Best for:** hitting protein without a spreadsheet. **Limitations:** subscription-only; daily food logging is a real habit cost, and it's a poor fit for anyone with a history of disordered eating.

## Goal 4: Cycle-aware training and tracking

### 5. Wild.AI — Best attempt at cycle-based programming

**Platform:** iOS &amp; Android · **Price:** free tier with a paid level · **iOS:** 4.4 stars, 396 ratings

Wild.AI takes your cycle phase (or hormonal contraception, or perimenopause status) and adjusts training, nutrition, and recovery guidance around it. The category is genuinely interesting and genuinely young — the underlying research on whether phase-based programming outperforms consistent training is limited, mixed, and complicated by huge individual variation.

Treat the recommendations as a structured starting hypothesis you test on yourself, rather than settled physiology. The lower rating and small review base reflect an early product, and it's worth knowing that going in.

**Best for:** systematically noticing how your cycle affects your training. **Limitations:** the science it's built on is unsettled; smallest user base on this list.

### 6. Flo — Best plain cycle tracker

**Platform:** iOS &amp; Android · **Price:** free tier with Premium · **iOS:** 4.7 stars, 1.9M+ ratings

If you want the cycle data without the training prescriptions, Flo is the default by a wide margin. Predictions, symptom logging, and a clean history you can hold up against your own training log to see whether your strong weeks cluster anywhere.

Worth checking the privacy settings and what you're comfortable storing, as with any health app handling reproductive data.

**Best for:** the raw cycle data, used however you want. **Limitations:** offers no training guidance; heavy content and Premium prompting.

## Goal 5: Recovery

### 7. Oura — Best recovery and sleep tracking

**Platform:** device + app · **Price:** ring purchase plus monthly membership · **iOS:** 4.9 stars, 271,000+ ratings

A ring is easier to sleep in than a watch, the battery runs for days, and the sleep staging is what most users rate highest. Oura also reports cycle-phase insights from temperature data, which is a reasonable use of the sensor.

Two honest caveats. Readiness scores are directional signals rather than instructions — a low score on a day you feel fine is information, not a rest day order. And it's the most expensive item here once you count the hardware and the membership together, so it's a later purchase rather than a first one.

**Best for:** sleep data and long-run recovery trends with minimal friction. **Limitations:** meaningful upfront cost plus subscription; readiness scoring is imprecise and easy to over-read.

## Goal 6: Training at home

### 8. Nike Training Club — Best free home workouts

**Platform:** iOS &amp; Android · **Price:** free · **iOS:** 4.8 stars, 279,000+ ratings

The whole library is free: guided strength, mobility, and conditioning sessions with video, plus structured multi-week programs. For starting out, travel weeks, or filling gaps between gym sessions, nothing else at this quality costs nothing.

The ceiling is the equipment. Bodyweight and light dumbbells will build strength for a while and then stop, because you run out of ways to add load. Use it to hold the habit, and add a barbell when muscle becomes the priority.

**Best for:** free, well-produced sessions with no equipment. **Limitations:** limited loading caps the hypertrophy ceiling; no progression tracking worth the name.

### 9. Down Dog — Best yoga and mobility

**Platform:** iOS &amp; Android · **Price:** free level with a subscription · **iOS:** 4.9 stars, 326,000+ ratings

Down Dog generates a new sequence every session rather than replaying the same recorded class, with control over length, pace, focus, and difficulty. The same studio produces barre, HIIT, and meditation versions.

This is a supplement rather than a strength program — yoga builds mobility and some endurance, and won't drive the muscle side of recomp. Paired with two lifting days it's an excellent third and fourth session.

**Best for:** mobility and recovery days you'll actually do. **Limitations:** not a strength stimulus; the free level is limited compared to the subscription.

### 10. Peloton — Best class experience without the bike

**Platform:** iOS &amp; Android · **Price:** app subscription tiers · **iOS:** 4.9 stars, 813,000+ ratings

The app-only tiers give you the instructor-led classes — strength, running, cycling, yoga — without owning any hardware, and the instruction quality is the reason people stay. If a voice and a schedule are what get you moving, this is the strongest version of that on the market.

The strength classes are follow-along, which means the load progression is on you to manage. Log the weights somewhere else if you want to know whether you're getting stronger.

**Best for:** motivation from real instruction and structure. **Limitations:** subscription; follow-along format makes progressive overload hard to track.

## So which two or three should you actually install?

Every app is a daily habit you're signing up for, and habits fail at a predictable rate. Pick for the next three months, not for a complete collection.

<div className="post-steps">

1. **New to lifting:** Ladder or Nike Training Club, alone, until training three times a week is automatic.
2. **Recomping:** a training log, a nutrition tracker, and weekly photos. That combination covers input, fuel, and output.
3. **Already lifting consistently:** Hevy plus MacroFactor. Add photo tracking when the scale stops telling you anything.
4. **Curious about your cycle:** add a tracker and read it against your training log for three cycles before changing your programming.

</div>

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best fitness app for women in 2026?

It depends on the goal. For strength training, Hevy for logging your own program or Ladder for a coached one. For nutrition, MacroFactor. For tracking visible change, a photo-analysis app like GainFrame. For home workouts, Nike Training Club is free and good. Two apps that fit your actual routine beat ten that don't.

### Do women need a fitness app designed specifically for women?

Only for the parts that are genuinely different — cycle tracking, pregnancy and postpartum programming, and pelvic floor work. Barbell training, progressive overload, and protein targets work the same way regardless of sex. A general app with a good training log will serve you better than a women's app whose main differentiator is the color scheme.

### Should you train differently around your menstrual cycle?

Possibly, though the evidence is genuinely mixed and individual variation is large. Some women report feeling strongest in the follicular phase and flatter in the late luteal phase; controlled research on whether phase-based programming outperforms consistent training is limited and inconsistent. Tracking your own energy and lifts for a few cycles tells you more than any general rule.

### What is the best free workout app for women?

Nike Training Club gives away its whole library — guided strength, mobility, and conditioning sessions plus multi-week programs, at no cost. Hevy's free tier covers routine building and progression logging indefinitely if you train in a gym. Down Dog's yoga and barre generators have a free level too. None of these require a subscription to be worth installing.

### How do you track progress when the scale isn't moving?

That's the normal case during recomposition, and it's why photos and measurements exist. Weekly progress photos in consistent lighting, waist and hip measurements, and your strength numbers will all show change the scale flattens. Menstrual-cycle water retention adds several pounds of noise on top, which makes week-to-week weigh-ins close to useless.
