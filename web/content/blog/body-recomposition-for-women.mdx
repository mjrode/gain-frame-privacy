<div className="post-callout post-quick-answer"><p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> Body recomposition means building muscle and losing fat over the same period. To follow your training, log a repeatable lift, a waist measurement and an optional progress photo for four weeks. Each describes a different part of your experience. Use the records together without assigning a target body-fat percentage or a deadline for visible change.</p></div>

Last month you could row a pair of dumbbells for eight reps. This month you can do ten with the same weight, but the bathroom scale shows the same number. The extra two reps tell you something specific. The scale alone can't tell you whether your muscle or fat mass changed.

## What does body recomposition look like in a training log?

A log records what happened in the gym. Pick a lift you can repeat with a similar setup, record the load and reps, and note anything that changed. “35 lb dumbbell row, 10 reps, same bench angle” is a better comparison than “back day felt easier.”

Researchers distinguish strength from muscle size by measuring them separately. In one 12-week study involving women and men, investigators tested elbow strength and also used MRI to measure the trained muscle. Individuals varied in their response. A set of rows in your own log gives you strength data. It cannot establish how much muscle tissue you gained. [Original study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15947721/)

## How do you build a routine you can compare?

Choose a resistance-training routine that fits your schedule. Record one or two exercises you expect to repeat, including your range of motion and the equipment you used. If a machine changes, note its settings. A busy week or an easier session belongs in the log too.

Regular meals, adequate energy for training and protein-containing foods support recovery. The [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/ExerciseAndAthleticPerformance-HealthProfessional/) summarizes sports-nutrition guidance on those basics. A restrictive diet or a fixed body-fat target is unnecessary for using this log. If you're deciding whether recomp fits your priorities, see our [bulk, cut or recomp guide](/blog/bulk-cut-or-recomp/).

Copy these four rows into a note or training journal. Use the same lift and side, the same waist landmark and the same units. If taking photos, choose the same lighting, camera height, distance, clothing and relaxed pose. Leave any field blank when you don't have a comparable reading.

**Example entry.** On November 2 you rowed 35 lb for eight reps on each side, recorded your waist at the top of the hip bone and saved a front photo taken before training. On November 9, you rowed the same load for ten. You forgot the tape and took a photo under different lights. Enter the ten reps and leave the comparison fields blank.

After a few entries, [review your recomp tracking signals](/tools/recomp-reality-checker/). Use the tool to put training, weight and waist trends in context. It cannot measure changes in fat or muscle tissue.

## What does a smaller waist or an unchanged weight mean?

A tape measures circumference. Its result depends on the landmark, posture, breath and tape tension. If the position moved between weeks, take the reading again at your recorded site before interpreting the change. The [body measurement guide](/blog/how-to-take-body-measurements/) shows the setup.

A scale reports total body weight. Fluid, food in the digestive tract and the timing of your weigh-in all affect that number. If you use it, compare readings taken under similar conditions. A stable weight alongside stronger lifts or a smaller waist is compatible with several explanations. It cannot confirm how much fat or muscle changed.

## How should you review the four weeks?

Start with your stated goal. If it was completing two lifting sessions per week, count the sessions. If it was adding reps, compare the same lift. Then look at the waist and photo entries you took under comparable conditions.

Keep the routine if it still fits, or write down one change you want to test during the next block. If your waist readings were hard to repeat, a better landmark is a more useful change than a new target number. ## What else should you know about women's recomp?

### What is body recomposition for women?

Body recomposition means gaining muscle and losing fat over the same period. Your scale measures the combined weight of everything in your body. A log of training performance, waist measurements and optionally photos can help you follow changes you care about, even when scale weight changes little.

### What body-fat percentage should I aim for?

You do not need a percentage target to begin tracking your training. A population average describes a group, and an illustration shows an example shape. Neither can determine which percentage suits your health or your sport. A specific performance goal gives you something you can measure directly.

### How often should I take progress photos during recomp?

Choose a schedule that's practical for you and use the same setup each time. The example log leaves room for one check-in per week, but you can use just the first and last rows for photos. Record dates, lighting and camera position so the pictures are comparable.

### How can I tell whether a strength increase means muscle gain?

You can record the extra reps or load as progress on that exercise. A new technique, more rest and familiarity with the lift can also affect the number. Keep the setup steady and look for repeat performances. A workout log cannot translate a strength gain into pounds of new muscle.

### How long should I wait before assessing my progress?

Four weeks gives you a first set of records to review. There is no deadline for seeing a visible change or an expected number of inches to lose. Use the first review to compare the sessions you completed, your performance and whether you can repeat the measurement setup.
