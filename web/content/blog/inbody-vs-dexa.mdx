Your gym's InBody says 16% body fat. A DEXA scan a few days later says 20%. You trained hard, skipped breakfast for one test, drank more water before the other, and now you have two expensive-looking reports that disagree.

That is the central InBody vs DEXA problem. Both methods can help you track body composition, but they measure different physical signals and calculate the final number differently. The most useful choice depends on whether you need a periodic reference scan or a convenient trend you can repeat under controlled conditions.

## Which is more accurate for body fat, InBody or DEXA?

DEXA is the stronger reference method for body-fat percentage and regional composition. It sends two low-energy X-ray beams through the body and estimates three compartments: bone mineral, fat mass, and lean soft tissue. Researchers commonly use DEXA as the criterion method when validating BIA devices.

InBody uses direct segmental, multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analysis. You stand barefoot on electrodes and hold hand electrodes while small electrical currents travel through five body segments. The device measures impedance, estimates body water, and derives fat-free mass and body fat from those measurements and physiological models.

The distinction between correlation and agreement matters. A device can track who is leaner or heavier very well while still returning a systematic offset from DEXA. In a study of **795 physically fit active-duty adults**, the InBody 770 showed high correlation with DEXA, yet its body-fat estimates had a **3.9 percentage-point mean absolute error** and averaged **3.4 points lower** than DEXA.

That result does not create a universal correction formula. It describes one device, one study population, and one protocol. Your local InBody model, DEXA machine, hydration, recent training, and body type can produce a different gap.

## How do InBody and DEXA measure your body differently?

The two methods begin with different raw signals.

**InBody measures electrical impedance.** Water-rich tissue conducts current differently from fat tissue. Multiple frequencies and separate arm, leg, and trunk measurements give professional InBody models more detail than a basic foot-only smart scale. The final fat and lean values are still derived estimates that depend on body-water assumptions.

**DEXA measures X-ray attenuation.** The scanner records how two X-ray energy levels pass through the body while you lie still. Software separates the image into bone mineral and soft-tissue regions, then estimates how much of the soft tissue is fat or lean.

DEXA does not directly weigh every muscle fiber. Its "lean" value includes water, organs, connective tissue, and other fat-free soft tissue. InBody's segmental lean output also should not be read as a pure measurement of contractile muscle.

## Why do InBody and DEXA give different results?

The reports disagree because each method has a different model and different sources of variation. The difference is expected, even when both tests are performed correctly.

For InBody, water distribution is central. Food, fluid, alcohol, a hard training session, using the bathroom, cold skin, and standing posture can change impedance. InBody's own preparation guidance emphasizes consistent conditions, bare hands and feet, removing heavy items, using the restroom, standing upright before testing, and staying still during the test.

DEXA also benefits from standardized conditions. Research reviews report that food, hydration, recent exercise, positioning, and the operator's region placement can influence lean and fat estimates. Results can also differ between manufacturers, machine generations, and software versions.

A scan performed on a different machine after a large meal is a weak comparison with a fasted baseline on another machine. The equipment may be excellent while the comparison is noisy.

DEXA's regional rows can help you compare left-to-right lean soft tissue and see where fat mass is concentrated. Android and gynoid regions describe abdominal versus hip and thigh distribution. Some systems also estimate visceral adipose tissue, but the report and facility should explain exactly how that value is derived.

The most valuable section is often the history table. A second scan on the same machine, under similar conditions, lets you compare changes in total mass, fat mass, and lean mass. One isolated scan describes a moment. A standardized series shows direction.

## Which test should you repeat for progress tracking?

Choose the method you can repeat consistently enough for your decision.

- **Choose DEXA** when you want a periodic reference, regional detail, or bone-mineral information to discuss with a qualified professional.
- **Choose InBody** when the same device is readily available and you want more frequent feedback without radiation or an appointment.
- **Use both carefully** when DEXA anchors the occasional baseline and InBody supplies a separate, higher-frequency trend between scans.

Keep each method in its own history. A 17% InBody result and a 20% DEXA result do not mean you gained three points. They are readings from different systems. Progress is the change from InBody to InBody or DEXA to DEXA under comparable conditions.

## How should you standardize your next InBody or DEXA test?

Use this quick checklist before repeat testing:

- Book the same time of day when possible.
- Use the same device and facility.
- Follow that facility's food and hydration instructions.
- Avoid a hard workout before the test when the protocol requests it.
- Use the bathroom beforehand.
- Wear similar lightweight clothing and remove external metal for DEXA.
- Record unusual factors such as travel, soreness, illness, or fluid retention.
- Compare the full report, including fat mass and lean mass, instead of reacting only to body-fat percentage.

Consistency cannot remove every source of error. It reduces the avoidable noise so a real change has a better chance of standing out.

## What did the InBody validation study leave unanswered?

The 795-person study provides useful evidence because it directly compared the InBody 770 with a GE Lunar iDXA in a large, physically fit sample. It did not validate every InBody model, every DEXA machine, or every body type. The participants were active-duty adults, so the findings may not transfer cleanly to older adults, people with obesity, or people with conditions that alter fluid balance.

The study also compared measurements at one point in time. A cross-sectional agreement result does not prove that the two methods detect small changes equally well during a bulk, cut, or recomp. Longitudinal tracking needs its own repeatability evidence and disciplined testing conditions.

## Which should you choose in 2026?

Use DEXA when the decision needs a stronger reference measurement, regional fat and lean estimates, or bone-mineral context. It costs more, takes an appointment, and works best as an occasional checkpoint.

Use InBody when access and repetition matter more. A professional multi-frequency device can produce a useful trend when you use the same machine and standardize hydration, food, exercise, and timing.

The practical framework is simple. Pick one primary method. Establish a repeatable protocol. Compare like with like. Use a second method as context instead of forcing its number into the first method's history.
