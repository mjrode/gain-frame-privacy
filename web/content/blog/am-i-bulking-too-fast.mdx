Six weeks into your bulk, you're up 9 pounds and your bench is up 10. The spreadsheet says winning. Then you catch your reflection getting out of the shower and the line of your stomach has gone soft in a way that 9 pounds of muscle would not explain — because 9 pounds of muscle in six weeks is not a thing your body can do.

That gap between what the scale added and what your muscles could plausibly have built is the entire question of this page. The check takes one line of arithmetic, and it's worth running monthly, because a bulk that's moving too fast doesn't feel like failure while it's happening. It feels like progress.

## How fast should a bulk actually move?

Your rate ceiling is set by how fast you can build muscle, plus a small allowance for the glycogen and water that come with more food and more training. The muscle numbers are the commonly cited coaching heuristics covered in depth in [how much muscle you can gain in a month](/blog/how-much-muscle-can-you-gain-in-a-month/):

| Training age | Plausible monthly muscle (men) | Sensible monthly scale-gain target |
|---|---|---|
| Beginner (year 1) | ~1–2 lb | ~2–3 lb |
| Intermediate (years 2–3) | ~0.5–1 lb, or ~0.25–0.5% of body weight | ~1–2 lb |
| Advanced (year 4+) | ~0.25 lb or less | ~0.5–1 lb |

Women's plausible muscle rates run at roughly half the male figures in absolute terms, so the scale-gain targets scale down accordingly.

Two caveats before you apply it. First, the opening weeks of any bulk add a one-time glycogen-and-water jump of several pounds that isn't muscle or fat — [why weight goes up when you start lifting](/blog/why-weight-goes-up-when-lifting/) breaks it down — so judge the rate from week three or four onward. Second, these are heuristics, not lab values; the point isn't the decimal, it's the order of magnitude.

## How do you run the rate check?

One comparison: **monthly scale gain versus plausible monthly muscle.** Whatever exceeds the plausible muscle rate — minus the early water allowance — is going somewhere other than muscle, and past week four that somewhere is fat.

Worked example. A 170-pound intermediate lifter, two years of consistent training, is gaining 4 pounds a month on his bulk. His plausible muscle rate at 0.25–0.5% of body weight per month is roughly 0.4 to 0.9 pounds. The check:

- **Monthly gain:** 4 lb
- **Plausible muscle:** ~0.5–1 lb, being generous
- **Everything else:** ~3+ lb per month — fat, at a pace of 35+ pounds a year

His lifts are going up, which is the part that makes this hard to believe from inside. But lifts go up in any surplus. The surplus was supposed to buy muscle at 1 pound a month, and he's paying for 4 — the other 3 are being delivered as fat, every month, on schedule.

The generalized red line: **if your monthly gain is more than about twice your plausible muscle rate, the majority of what you're gaining is fat.** For our example lifter, that line sits around 2 pounds a month. He's at double it.

## What are the warning signs you're bulking too fast?

The scale check is the math. These four signs are how it shows up on your body — commonly, before you're willing to believe the math.

**Your waist is climbing faster than your lifts.** The tape at your navel is the most honest instrument you own. A waist adding half an inch or more per month while your lifts inch up at normal-progression speed means the surplus is landing around your middle, not in your muscles.

**Monthly gain is more than twice your plausible muscle rate.** The red line from the worked example. Cross it for one month and it might be water or noise; cross it for two consecutive months and it's a trend with only one available explanation.

**Definition is vanishing in your photos.** Losing some crispness on a bulk is normal and expected. Watching the line of your shoulders, arms, or midsection blur noticeably month over month is the visual version of the waist tape — fat arriving faster than muscle underneath it.

**Clothes get tight at the waist first.** Muscle fills sleeves, chest, and thighs. Fat, for most men, fills the waistband. If your jeans complained before your shirts did, the bulk is telling you where the weight went.

## What should you do if you're gaining too fast?

**Trim the surplus, don't torch it.** Cut your daily intake by 100 to 200 calories, keep training identical, and re-check the trend after two or three weeks of weekly averages. That's usually enough to pull a runaway rate back inside the target band while keeping the muscle-building conditions intact.

The instinct to panic-cut — slashing 500-plus calories the day you notice — mostly backfires. It throws away the training momentum and surplus the bulk existed to create, usually in reaction to one bad mirror day rather than a trend. Adjust like a thermostat, not a fire alarm.

The exception is when it's already gone far: body fat up several percentage points, waist well past where you're comfortable, months of fast gaining banked. ## How do you track a bulk so this doesn't happen again?

Three instruments, none optional, because each one fails alone: the scale can't see composition, the tape can't see the whole body, and un-matched photos can't be compared honestly.

<div className="post-steps">

1. **Weekly average weight.** Weigh daily under matched conditions, average by week, compare weekly averages. Single weigh-ins swing 2–5 pounds on water and gut contents — more than a month of muscle signal.
2. **Monthly waist at the navel.** Same conditions each time, morning is least noisy. This is the instrument that catches a fat-heavy bulk earliest.
3. **Matched monthly photos.** Same pose, same lighting, same time of day. Photos catch the definition drift that the scale rationalizes away.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much weight gain per month is too fast on a bulk?

Compare against your plausible muscle rate. Commonly cited heuristics put intermediates around 0.25 to 0.5 percent of body weight per month and beginners up to roughly 1 to 2 pounds. A useful red line: monthly gain more than about twice your plausible muscle rate means the majority of what you're adding is almost certainly fat.

### Is gaining 2 pounds a week too fast when bulking?

For almost everyone, yes — dramatically. Two pounds a week is roughly 8 pounds a month, while commonly cited muscle rates top out around 2 pounds a month for beginners and less for everyone else. Past the first weeks of a bulk, when glycogen and water inflate the scale, a sustained 2-pound-per-week trend is mostly fat accumulation.

### Should I cut if I bulked too fast?

Usually not immediately. If you caught it within a month or two, trim your surplus by 100 to 200 calories and let the rate normalize — a panic cut throws away the training momentum the bulk was building. A short dedicated mini cut makes sense when the fat gain has gone further, commonly around the point where body fat has climbed several percentage points.

### How do I know if my bulk weight is muscle or fat?

No scale can tell you, so triangulate. Weekly-average weight gives the rate, a monthly waist measurement shows where the weight is landing, and matched progress photos show whether definition is holding. Lifts climbing while the waist holds roughly steady suggests muscle; a waist growing faster than the lifts is the classic fat-gain signature.

### Can beginners bulk faster than intermediates?

Yes — commonly cited heuristics allow beginners roughly 1 to 2 pounds of monthly gain against 0.5 to 1 for intermediates, because untrained muscle grows fastest. Beginners also get a one-time glycogen and water jump early on that inflates the scale beyond tissue. The check is the same at every level: compare the monthly trend to the plausible muscle rate for your training age.
