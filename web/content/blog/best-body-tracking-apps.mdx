You searched for a body tracking app and got a list of thirty. Half of them track workouts, a few analyze photos, one wants you to buy a suit with dots on it, and three are scales with an app attached. They are all called body trackers and they measure completely different things.

That's the actual problem with this category. The rankings assume you already know which kind of tool you want, when the question most people have is one level up: what should I be tracking in the first place? This page sorts the field by tracking type, tells you what each type can and can't see, and sends you to the deep-dive comparison for whichever one matches your goal.

## What are the six types of body tracking app?

Every app in this space measures one of six things. Everything else is packaging.

The two columns that matter for choosing: the first four types track your *body*, the last two track your *behavior*. A body-side tracker tells you whether something changed. A behavior-side tracker tells you why. Running one of each is the whole stack for most people, which we lay out in full in our [2026 fitness app stack guide](/blog/best-fitness-app-stack-2026/).

## Which app tracks visible physique change?

### 1\. GainFrame — Best for scored progress photos

**Type:** Photo / visual AI · **Platform:** [iOS App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/gainframe-progress-photos/id6759252082) · **Price:** Free (25 photos lifetime), Pro $5.99/mo or $39.99/yr

If your goal has a visual outcome — leaner waist, wider shoulders, visible abs — then the thing worth tracking is what a photo shows. [GainFrame](https://gainframe.app) takes a single check-in photo and returns an estimated body fat percentage, FFMI, a 1–100 physique score, and ratings for 12 individual muscle groups, all plotted over time.

The muscle-group breakdown is what separates this type from the rest: a scale can tell you that you lost four pounds, but only the visual layer tells you your delts held while your waist came in. Side-by-side comparison across any two check-ins, a Deep Dive Compare report, and Smart Import for old camera-roll photos round it out.

**Best for:** anyone whose goal is a look rather than a number. **Limitations:** iOS only; the readings are AI estimates from an image, not clinical measurement; the free tier stops at 25 photos.

### 2\. Apple Photos — Best free floor for this category

**Type:** Photo, unscored · **Platform:** iOS · **Price:** Free

Worth naming because it's genuinely sufficient for some people. A hidden album, the same spot on the same wall, same time of day, once a week. You get zero analysis and zero trend data, but you get the raw evidence, and raw evidence beats an app you stopped opening in March. Our guide on [how often to take progress photos](/blog/how-often-progress-photos/) covers the protocol either way.

**Best for:** people who want the habit before the tooling. **Limitations:** no scoring, no trend, no comparison view, and it's on you to stay consistent.

## Which apps estimate body composition from a scan?

### 3\. Spren — Best for periodic composition check-ins

**Type:** Composition scan (front/back photo AI) · **Platform:** iOS + Android · **Price:** Subscription, free tier limited by scans per month

Spren is built around the deliberate session rather than the daily habit: form-fitting clothing, controlled lighting, front and back photo, and out comes a body fat percentage and lean mass estimate. It's cross-platform, which matters if you're on Android, and it's the most established name in the photo-based composition lane.

**Best for:** structured monthly composition assessments on either platform. **Limitations:** setup discipline is real — inconsistent conditions produce readings you can't compare; no FFMI or per-muscle output. Full breakdown in our [Spren app review](/blog/spren-app-review/).

### 4\. Recomp AI — Best for recomposition-framed scanning

**Type:** Composition scan (3D model) · **Platform:** iOS · **Price:** Free download with subscription tiers

Recomp AI, from ISO Labs, scans with your phone camera and returns a 3D model alongside body fat, lean mass, and regional circumference estimates, all framed around losing fat and building muscle at the same time. It answers "what am I right now" better than it answers "is this working," which is the trade every scan-first app makes.

**Best for:** people mid-recomp who want a fresh composition snapshot each session. **Limitations:** newer app with a shorter public track record; pricing has moved, so check the current listing. See the [Recomp AI comparison](/blog/recomp-ai-vs-gainframe/) for detail.

## Which app gives you actual measurements?

### 5\. MeThreeSixty — Best for circumference numbers

**Type:** Measurement + 3D avatar · **Platform:** iOS + Android · **Price:** Free scans with a five-scan history cap; Premium around $5/mo

Built by Size Stream, a company that makes professional scanning hardware, MeThreeSixty turns two poses into a smoothed 3D avatar with 14+ estimated circumferences — waist, hip, chest, thigh. If what you need is inches for clothing fit, a competition class, or a waist-to-height calculation, this is the type to use.

The avatar is a shape mesh, so it captures proportion and circumference while showing nothing about muscle separation or leanness. That's the category's honest boundary, and it's why measurement apps and photo apps pair well rather than compete. Full head-to-head in our [MeThreeSixty comparison](/blog/methreesixty-vs-gainframe/).

**Best for:** tracking waist and limb circumference without a tape. **Limitations:** tight clothing and consistent posture required; smoothed avatar hides definition; free history caps at five scans.

## Which scale app should you use for weight and body fat?

### 6\. Renpho and Hume Body Pod — Best for automated weight trends

**Type:** Weight + BIA · **Platform:** hardware plus iOS/Android app · **Price:** ~$20–35 (Renpho) to ~$229 (Hume Body Pod)

The scale's job is the daily weight number, which it measures directly and precisely, synced without you doing anything. Renpho does that for the price of a pizza order and is the reason this category has a floor. The Hume Body Pod adds hand electrodes on top of foot electrodes, which meaningfully improves the composition read compared with foot-only scales.

The body fat percentage on any BIA scale is modeled from electrical resistance, and resistance moves with your hydration. A salty dinner or a hard leg session can swing the estimate several points overnight, which is why the number is a long-run trend and never a daily readout. We cover the physics in [are smart scales accurate](/blog/are-smart-scales-accurate/) and the honest alternative in [tape measure vs smart scale](/blog/tape-measure-vs-smart-scale/).

**Best for:** effortless daily weight data. **Limitations:** body fat readings are hydration-sensitive; don't compare readings across two different scales. Reviews: [Renpho](/blog/renpho-scale-review/) and [Hume Body Pod](/blog/hume-body-pod-review/).

## Which apps track the behaviors driving the change?

### 7\. Hevy — Best for the strength signal

**Type:** Workout log · **Platform:** iOS + Android · **Price:** Free tier covers most people; Pro optional

Your training log is the earliest indicator you have. Lifts climbing through a bulk means the stimulus is there; lifts sliding at equal effort during a cut is a warning that arrives before any scan or photo shows it. Hevy's free tier is complete enough that paying is optional, and it's the category benchmark for a reason — see [Hevy vs Strong](/blog/hevy-vs-strong/) for the head-to-head.

**Best for:** the leading indicator on muscle. **Limitations:** indirect — strength dips for sleep, stress, and calorie reasons too.

### 8\. MacroFactor — Best for the intake side

**Type:** Nutrition log · **Platform:** iOS + Android · **Price:** ~$12/mo

Nothing on the body side moves without the intake side. MacroFactor's adaptive expenditure model adjusts your targets from your own weight and logging data instead of asking you to guess a maintenance number, which is the feature that makes it worth a subscription over the free loggers. Our [MacroFactor review](/blog/macrofactor-review/) has the full picture.

**Best for:** hitting protein and calorie targets you can verify. **Limitations:** subscription-only; daily logging is a real habit cost.

## Why do you probably need two apps, not one?

Every app on this list has a blind spot that another category fills. The scale can't see shape. The photo app can't see what you ate. The workout log can't see body fat. The scan app can't tell you whether your delts grew.

Chasing a single app that does all six ends badly — the all-in-one products in this space tend to be mediocre at five jobs to be adequate at one. The stack that works is smaller than people expect:

<div className="post-steps">

1. **Pick one body-side tracker** based on your goal. Visual goal, use a photo app. Numbers goal, use a scan or measurement app. Weight goal, use a scale.
2. **Pick one behavior-side tracker.** Lifting is your priority, use a workout log. Fat loss is your priority, use a nutrition log.
3. **Set a fixed check-in cadence.** Weekly for photos and measurements, daily for weight and logs, monthly for a full comparison.
4. **Add a third app only when you've used the first two for eight weeks** and can name the question they aren't answering.

</div>

<div className="post-callout"><p><strong>The pattern behind all of it:</strong> consistency beats precision in this category. A moderately noisy reading you collect 52 times a year tells a clearer story than a precise one you collect twice. Whichever type you pick, standardize the conditions and read the trend line rather than the individual data point.</p></div>

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best body tracking app?

There isn't one — the category covers six different jobs. For visible physique change, a photo-analysis app like GainFrame. For circumference numbers, a 3D scan app like MeThreeSixty. For weight trends, any BIA scale app. For strength, Hevy. For intake, MacroFactor. Pick by the question you're trying to answer, then add a second app only if it answers a different one.

### Do I need more than one body tracking app?

Most people end up with two, and the pairing that works is one body-side tracker plus one behavior-side tracker. The body side tells you what changed — photos, composition estimates, weight. The behavior side tells you why — training volume and food intake. One app from each column covers almost every tracking need without app fatigue.

### What's the difference between a body tracking app and a fitness tracker?

Fitness trackers — Apple Watch, Fitbit, Garmin — measure activity: steps, heart rate, sleep, calories burned. Body tracking apps measure the result of that activity: body fat estimates, muscle change, circumference, weight. Activity data tells you what you did; body data tells you whether it worked. They overlap less than the shared shelf space suggests.

### Are AI body tracking apps accurate?

They produce estimates, not measurements, and the honest framing is that every consumer method does. One peer-reviewed study of AI photo estimation reported strong agreement with DEXA across a large adult sample, which is encouraging without being proof for any single app. Judge them on whether the direction they report matches what you can see over months.

### Is there a free body tracking app that actually works?

Several. Hevy's free tier covers workout logging completely. MeThreeSixty scans free with a five-scan history cap. GainFrame's free tier includes 25 AI-scored photos on iOS. Apple Photos with a hidden album costs nothing at all for plain progress photos. The free options are strongest for logging and weakest for composition estimates, which is where subscriptions cluster.
