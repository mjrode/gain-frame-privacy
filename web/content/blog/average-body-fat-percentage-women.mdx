<div className="post-callout post-quick-answer"><p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> US women aged 20 and older averaged 39.9% body fat in the 1999 to 2004 NHANES whole-body DXA survey. The mean was 37.8% at ages 20 to 39 and 42.4% at ages 60 to 79. These historical population averages describe the women surveyed. They do not set a personal fitness goal.</p></div>

Say your scale reads 31%. Before you compare that number with a chart, look for three things on the chart: who was measured, the year and the method. A 31% reading from a home scale and a 31% result from a whole-body scan come from different measurements.

## What was the average body fat percentage for women by age?

The National Center for Health Statistics published the following figures for female participants in the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Examiners used whole-body dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, usually abbreviated DXA or DEXA. The original table reports age bands of 20 years for most adults. It cannot give a separate average for ages 30 to 39 or 50 to 59. [NCHS Table 3, printed page 12](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/series/sr_11/sr11_250.pdf#page=18)

| Age in years | Participants | Mean body fat | Median | Middle half (25th to 75th percentile) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 20 to 39 | 2,043 | 37.8% | 37.8% | 32.5% to 42.9% |
| 40 to 59 | 2,049 | 40.5% | 41.0% | 36.4% to 45.1% |
| 60 to 79 | 1,846 | 42.4% | 42.8% | 38.9% to 46.2% |
| 80 and older | 594 | 40.4% | 41.0% | 36.9% to 44.4% |
| All adults 20 and older | 6,532 | 39.9% | 40.5% | 35.4% to 44.7% |

The last row uses the report's unadjusted overall age distribution. Percentages are survey-weighted. The participant counts tell you how many people contributed in each age group. The survey used statistical imputation to account for missing scan data.

## How should you read a number like 40.5%?

For women aged 40 to 59, 40.5% was the **mean**, the average across that age group. The **median** was 41.0%, the point that divided the group in half. A woman with a DXA result of 38% would have fallen within the middle half of that historical group. That comparison says nothing about her strength, symptoms or training needs.

The chart compares different groups of women examined in 1999 to 2004. No one in the table was followed from her 20s to her 80s. The differences between rows cannot tell you how an individual woman's body will change. For more context on changes over time, see our [guide to menopause and body composition](/blog/menopause-body-composition/).

## How do fitness categories compare with survey averages?

A fitness chart might label a band “athletic” or “average.” Check its source and who defined those labels. A label describes a category selected by the chart's authors. The NHANES table answers how total body fat was distributed in a measured population, including people with different training backgrounds.

If you're tracking a sport, a recent race time or a set performed at a recorded load tells you more about that particular performance. If you have a health concern about a scan result, the clinician who ordered it can interpret it with your history. There is no way to read a woman's individual health status from an age-group mean.

## How do you compare your own readings over time?

Write down the instrument and date with each result. If you switch from a smart scale to a scan or start using a different scale, mark the change in your log. Treat the first reading with the new method as a new baseline. The [comparison of measurement methods](/blog/every-way-to-measure-body-fat/) explains what each uses.

Dated photos can record appearance alongside a training log. Use the same light, camera position and pose. A [photo estimate](/tools/body-fat-from-photo/) is a separate observation whose uncertainty depends on the image and the method. Keep the original measurement alongside it.

## What else should you know about the age chart?

### What was the average body fat percentage for women in the US survey?

For US women aged 20 and older, NHANES reported a mean of 39.9% and a median of 40.5% in its 1999 to 2004 DXA data. The mean pooled the adult population as surveyed. The age-specific rows are more useful when you want to compare a particular age group.

### What was the average percentage for women in their 30s?

The published table combines ages 20 to 39. That group's mean was 37.8%, and its median was also 37.8%. The report does not give a separate estimate for just the 30s in this table. The group also includes women in their 20s.

### What does the middle half in the table mean?

The lower and upper numbers are the 25th and 75th percentiles. For example, among women aged 40 to 59, the middle half of the survey distribution ran from 36.4% to 45.1%. Those endpoints describe where people fell in the dataset. They are not clinical cutoffs.

### Can I compare a smart scale reading with the DXA chart?

Start by recording the method that produced your reading. The survey estimated body composition from whole-body DXA scans. A home scale estimates it from impedance and a device-specific formula. Differences between the two readings can reflect how the instruments work, even if your body has stayed the same.

### Can a percentage tell me how fit I am?

A total body-fat percentage describes one aspect of body composition. It does not capture how far you can run, the weight you can lift, where fat is distributed or how well you recover. Track measures that relate directly to the activity or health question you care about.
