Why I Added In-App Chat to GainFrame

Analytics tells me what 1,000 users do. I still had no idea why. So I opened a direct line and started asking.

A few months ago I wrote about the moment my analytics completely rewrote my roadmap. Data from 92 users told me things I never would have guessed: a third of people never uploaded a single photo, one onboarding step was silently killing 15% of signups, and the AI features I was most proud of were barely being touched.

That data was invaluable. But it had a ceiling.

Numbers tell you what happened. They do not tell you why. And at 1,000 users, the most important thing I can do right now is understand the why.

What Data Can't Tell You

I can see that a user opened the Compare tab 47 times in 30 days and never tapped Deep Dive once. What I cannot see is whether they don't know Deep Dive exists, or they looked at it once and found it overwhelming, or they simply don't care about body fat percentages — they just want to see if they look different.

Those three users require completely different responses from me as a builder. One needs better discoverability. One needs a clearer explanation. One is telling me something important about who my actual core user is versus who I thought they were.

Analytics gives me the signal. Conversation gives me the interpretation.

Why 1,000 Users Is the Right Time to Do This

There is a specific window in a product's life when talking directly to users is not just useful — it is the highest-leverage thing you can do. That window is early.

At 100 users, you are still figuring out if anyone wants what you built. At 10,000 users, the volume of feedback becomes noise. At 1,000 users, you have real people with real routines who have made a real decision to keep coming back. Their opinions are informed. Their frustrations are specific. Their suggestions are grounded in actual use.

I will not be able to personally reply to every message forever. I know that. But right now I can, and the things I learn in these conversations will shape every product decision I make for the next year. That is worth the time.

What I Actually Want to Know

I am not opening this channel to collect feature requests. I am opening it to understand three things:

  • Does user sentiment align with the metrics? If my data says the Compare feature is the most-used screen, do users actually think it's the most valuable part of the app? Or are they using it because everything else is harder to find?
  • What is working that I am not measuring? Some of the most important value a product delivers is invisible to analytics. Motivation, accountability, the feeling of progress. I want to hear people say it in their own words.
  • What frustrated them enough to notice? Not every frustration becomes feedback. Most people silently stop using something. The ones who take the time to say something are giving me a signal I should take seriously.

The Problem With User Feedback: Everyone Thinks They're Priority One

Here is the honest risk of opening a direct feedback channel: every request will feel urgent.

A user messages you that they wish they could export their photos as a PDF. It sounds easy. Another user wants a dark mode widget. Another wants workout log integration. Each message comes from a real person with a real need, and it is psychologically very hard to read those messages without wanting to build everything immediately.

That is the trap. Implementing every suggestion does not make a better product. It makes an incoherent one.

One message is an anecdote. Five messages about the same thing is a pattern. A pattern is worth exploring. A pattern that also shows up in your data is worth prioritizing.

The framework I am using is simple: I am logging every piece of feedback, but I am not reacting to any individual request in isolation. I am looking for the same frustration or need surfacing across multiple conversations, especially when it maps to something I can already see in the behavioral data. That convergence — when what users say aligns with what the data shows — is the signal I am actually looking for.

What This Looks Like in Practice

Starting today, there is a banner on the GainFrame home screen that lets you tap directly to chat with me. It is powered by Crisp, which means you are talking to a real person — not a bot, not a support ticket queue. If you send a message, I will read it and reply.

The banner is dismissible. I am not trying to interrupt your workout tracking. But if something is broken, something confused you, or you genuinely wish the app did something it does not do — I want to know.

Real human, real reply. That is the entire pitch.

The Rules I'm Setting for Myself

To make sure this is actually useful and does not become a reactive feature factory, I am following a few personal rules:

Log everything, react to nothing alone

Every message gets categorized. No single message changes the roadmap on its own.

Look for patterns, not requests

The same frustration mentioned by 5 people is infinitely more important than 5 different one-off ideas.

Cross-reference with data

When sentiment and behavioral data point at the same thing, that is the thing I build next.

Be honest about what I won't build

Not every good idea fits GainFrame. Saying no clearly is more respectful than letting a request disappear.

What I'm Hoping to Learn

By the end of this experiment, I want to be able to answer a question I cannot currently answer from my dashboard alone: who is GainFrame actually for?

I have hypotheses. People who want to see their physique change over time. People who are frustrated that the scale lies. People who need a record of their progress to stay motivated when results feel slow. But I do not know which of those is my real core user — the person who would be genuinely upset if GainFrame disappeared tomorrow.

Talking to users is how you find out. And then you build obsessively for that person.

A Simple Framework for Founder-Led Feedback

If you're building something with fewer than 10,000 users and haven't opened a direct channel yet, here is where to start:

  1. Pick one tool. Crisp, Intercom, or even a dedicated email address. Don't overthink it — the channel matters less than having one.
  2. Surface it prominently. A buried feedback link gets no messages. Put it somewhere users actually see it, like the app home screen.
  3. Log every message you get immediately. Notion, a spreadsheet, even a notes file. Categorize it: bug, confusion, feature request, praise.
  4. Look for convergence. After 30–50 messages, read through everything. What comes up more than twice? That is your signal.
  5. Cross-reference with behavioral data. If the same theme shows up in both conversations and analytics, move it to the top of your roadmap.
