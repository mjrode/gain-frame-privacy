<h2>2. Hydrostatic (Underwater) Weighing</h2>

<p>You exhale all the air from your lungs and get submerged in a tank of water while sitting on a
scale. Because fat is less dense than lean tissue, your underwater weight reveals your body
composition. Simple physics — but deeply uncomfortable.</p>

<h2>3. Bod Pod (Air Displacement Plethysmography)</h2>

<p>You sit inside an egg-shaped chamber while it measures the volume of air your body displaces.
Same density principle as hydrostatic weighing, but with air instead of water. No dunking
required.</p>

<!-- ===== METHOD 4: CALIPERS ===== -->
<h2>4. Skinfold Calipers</h2>

<p>A trained person pinches your skin at 3–7 specific sites (tricep, suprailiac, thigh, abdomen,
etc.) and measures the thickness of the fold in millimeters. Those numbers get plugged into a
formula that estimates total body fat.</p>

<!-- ===== METHOD 5: SMART SCALES ===== -->
<h2>5. Smart Scales (Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis)</h2>

<p>You step on a scale. It sends a tiny electrical current up one leg and down the other. Because
muscle holds more water than fat, the scale infers your body composition from the electrical
resistance.</p>

obese individuals.</p>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> Tracking weight trends (not body fat). The scale number is reliable.
The body fat percentage it displays is not.</p>

<h2>6. AI Body Fat Estimator from Photo</h2>

<p>You take a front photo and a side photo with your phone. An AI vision model analyzes muscle
definition, subcutaneous fat distribution, and body geometry — then cross-references your
height, weight, and age to estimate your body fat percentage.</p>

<p>This is the newest method in body composition analysis, and it's the one most people overlook.</p>

BIA scales typically score around 0.91 in similar studies. These results are promising, though they
should be viewed in context: the study tested under controlled lab conditions, and more independent
replication would strengthen the evidence.</p>

patterns, and leanness indicators — that trained humans have used for decades. It then
factors in your biometric data to produce an estimate with a built-in confidence score.</p>
</div>

<p><strong>Best for:</strong> Anyone who wants frequent, consistent body composition tracking
without scheduling appointments or buying equipment. Standardize your setup (same lighting, same
distance, same clothing)
and the results become more stable over time. Especially powerful for tracking
recomposition (losing fat while gaining muscle), where scale weight stays flat but your
physique is visibly changing.</p>

<!-- ===== ACCURACY VS CONSISTENCY ===== -->
<h2>Accuracy vs Consistency: What Actually Matters</h2>

<p>Among body fat measurement methods, consistency often beats occasional "perfect" tests.
A method you use regularly delivers more actionable data than a theoretically superior
method you rarely access.</p>

<p>Frequent measurements catch problems early. If your body fat starts creeping up, you
want to know in week two, not month six. Weekly data creates visible feedback that
supports the behaviors driving change. The absolute number matters less than the
trajectory.</p>

<div class="post-callout">
<p><strong>The key insight:</strong> Whether a method says you're at 14% or 15% is less
important than whether that number is consistently dropping over eight weeks. A $300
DEXA scan once a year tells you less about your transformation than weekly tracking
showing a clear, steady trend.</p>
</div>

<h3
style="font-family: var(--font-display); font-size: 1.2rem; font-weight: 700; margin: var(--sp-xl) 0 var(--sp-md);">
Standardization Checklist</h3>

<p>Whatever method you choose, consistency requires a repeatable protocol:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>BIA scales:</strong> Same time each morning, after using the bathroom,
before eating or drinking.</li>
<li><strong>AI photos:</strong> Same lighting, same distance from camera, same minimal
clothing, same time of day.</li>
<li><strong>Calipers:</strong> Same technician, same sites in same order, same pinch
technique.</li>
<li><strong>All methods:</strong> Record conditions (sleep, stress, training phase) to
contextualize outlier readings.</li>
</ul>

<!-- ===== WHICH METHOD ===== -->
<h2>Your Body Fat Tracking Framework</h2>

<p>There's no single best method. There's the best method <em>for your situation</em>.
The smart approach combines a baseline method with a tracking method, then compares
them periodically.</p>

<ol class="post-steps">
<li><strong>Pick your baseline method.</strong> If you can afford a DEXA scan once or
twice a year, do it. It gives you the most defensible absolute number and shows
regional fat distribution. If not, a two-photo AI estimate or calipers from the
same skilled technician provide a reasonable alternative.</li>
<li><strong>Pick your tracking method.</strong> This is the one you'll use weekly or
biweekly. It must be convenient and repeatable. AI photo estimation works for many
people because it's less sensitive to hydration than BIA and requires no equipment.
Calipers work too — but only from the same person.</li>
<li><strong>Watch the trend, not the number.</strong> Pick one tool, standardize your
process, and track the trajectory. Single readings carry uncertainty. Multi-week
averages tell you what's actually happening.</li>
<li><strong>Reassess every 8–12 weeks.</strong> Compare your tracking method against your
baseline. If the trend lines agree, you're on track. If they diverge, recalibrate.</li>
</ol>

<div class="post-callout">
<p><strong>Quick decision guide:</strong> Want maximum accuracy? Get a DEXA scan for
your baseline. Want weekly consistency without hydration variables? Try AI photos
under standardized conditions. Only have a smart scale? Use it under strict morning
conditions and focus on multi-week averages, not daily readings.</p>
</div>
